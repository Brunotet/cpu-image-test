[   0.0-   7.4] Here's something weird about English. A cow becomes beef, a pig becomes pork, a sheep becomes
[   7.4-  14.8] mutton, and a deer becomes venison. But a chicken? It's just chicken. So why does the animal get
[  14.8-  21.6] one name and the meat a totally different one? It all goes back to 1066. Back then,
[  21.6-  27.1] England spoke old English, and just across the channel sat Normandy, ruled by a duke
[  27.1-  33.1] named William, who was convinced the English crown belonged to him. So he built a fleet,
[  33.1-  38.6] sailed over, beat King Harold at the Battle of Hastings, and on Christmas Day, he was crowned
[  38.6-  45.0] King of England. Within a few years, the people at the top had changed. The king, the lords,
[  45.0-  51.4] the courts, they all spoke French, and they kept speaking it for around 300 years. But down on
[  51.4-  59.0] the farms, the people raising the animals still spoke English. Cow, pig, sheep, deer. The meat,
[  59.0-  64.3] though, often ended up on the tables of French-speaking lords, and their words for it were
[  64.3-  71.1] buff, poor, mutton, and venison. Over the next couple of centuries, those words slipped into
[  71.1-  78.0] English as beef, pork, mutton, and venison. And chicken? Chickens were cheap, and pretty much
[  78.0-  83.8] everyone kept them, rich or poor. So its old English name never had to change.
